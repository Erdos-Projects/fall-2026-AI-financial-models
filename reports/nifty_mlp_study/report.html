Nifty options: MLP baseline architecture comparison

Weekly check-in · October 22, 2026 · Completed experiment

We compared eight MLP architectures across three seeds each using the same 15 prepared features and chronological split. Fifteen fits are new; nine are verified results from the October 14 study. Wide / tapered has the lowest mean validation date-MAE among the MLPs (0.0149090). The predeclared complexity rule selects Compact, with 1,057 parameters, because its mean is only 0.142% above the best neural mean. Persistence has date-MAE 0.0148986; the best MLP's error is 0.070% higher. These are development-set results; the final test partition was not evaluated.

1. How last week's data exploration informs the models

The EDA audited 17,611,169 selected rows. Preprocessing retained 17,576,228 clean bars and produced 11,469,619 supervised examples. Prices and volumes are skewed; the retained data include 374,544 zero-volume bars, 490,741 prices at or below one price unit, and 3,345,913 flat OHLC bars. Missing minutes are common and are not filled. The unverified ninth field and inferred expiry dates are excluded. See the existing EDA summary and preprocessing summary.

The task is next-minute option log-return forecasting: y = log(close[t+1] / close[t]). Each observation uses six consecutive bars ending at t, and the target must occur exactly one minute later within the same source file and date. Inputs are 15 engineered tabular features, not a 6 × 15 raw sequence: logged price/strike/volume, range and candle change, one- and five-minute returns, recent volatility and volume, cyclical time, and call/put, zero-volume, low-price, and flat-bar indicators. Nine continuous features use training-only standardization; the others retain their encoding.

This representation supports small MLPs directly. Correlated windows and options reduce the effective sample size, motivating dropout and compact capacity despite millions of rows. Extreme returns motivate a robust training loss and reporting both absolute and squared errors. Sparse and low-price observations motivate subgroup checks. The dataset lacks verified complete contract identities, expiry inputs, synchronized underlying prices, and executable quotes; this experiment does not measure theoretical pricing error or trading profitability.

2. Baselines and literature-backed architecture choices

Persistence predicts zero return, equivalent to the next close equaling the current close. Ridge regression is a linear comparator using the same features; its penalty is selected from 0.0001, 0.01, and 1.0. The compact MLP is the established neural baseline against which the other MLPs are compared.

Plain MLP and residual tabular networks are established comparison families in Gorishniy et al. (2021). We test ordinary ReLU networks at different depths and widths, a matched dropout comparison, BatchNorm, and an adapted residual network. The exact widths below are project choices; they are not claims to reproduce published tuned architectures.

All heads are unconstrained linear scalars, allowing positive and negative returns. Ordinary hidden blocks are Linear → ReLU → Dropout; BatchNorm is inserted between Linear and ReLU. The residual model has a 15 → 64 ReLU stem and two blocks computing ReLU(h + Linear(Dropout(ReLU(Linear(LayerNorm(h)))))), followed by a scalar head. Each block contains two width-64 dense layers. This LayerNorm design adapts the residual family; the cited tabular ResNet uses BatchNorm instead.

Dropout is motivated by Srivastava et al. (2014); normalization choices by Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) and Ba et al. (2016). The standard/plain pair holds dense widths fixed while adding dropout. The standard/BatchNorm pair adds normalization at the same widths. Other comparisons change several architectural properties and parameter counts. The literature companion explains expected performance and cost tradeoffs and provides eight primary references verified online.

3. Controlled training and selection

  • Full training and validation arrays for every model; no downsampled results.
  • Seeds 17, 42, 2026, batch size 4,096, AdamW learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.0001, gradient norm clipping 1.0.
  • Smooth L1 loss (beta=1) on returns divided by their training-only standard deviation 0.0798147523, without centering or clipping.
  • At most 12 epochs; stop after three epochs without date-MAE improvement of at least 1e-6. Halve learning rate on a validation plateau, with patience one and minimum learning rate 1e-5. Restore the best eligible checkpoint.
  • Default PyTorch hidden initialization and zero output weights/bias, giving a persistence prediction before training. All training rows are shuffled within the training partition each epoch.
  • Primary metric: compute MAE on each validation date, then average equally across dates. Secondary metrics pool rows: return MAE/RMSE and reconstructed price MAE/RMSE, where predicted_price = close[t] * exp(predicted_return).
  • Selection: smallest parameter count within 0.5% of the lowest three-seed mean date-MAE; predeclared seed 42 is the reference checkpoint.

The protocol was saved before fitting and fixes these choices. The old nine runs are reused only after validating their artifact and input hashes; their provenance remains visible in runs.csv. This avoids claiming they were trained again. The extension leaves the prior study and its frozen checkpoint unchanged. Interrupted work is resumed only after checking saved run hashes; a partially completed seed is restarted.

This is a common-recipe comparison, not a separately optimized hyperparameter search for every family. Training loss weights rows equally, whereas selection weights dates equally. Ridge uses squared loss, so ridge-versus-MLP results compare complete pipelines rather than isolating architecture alone.

Accuracy versus complexity. The selected Compact has mean date-MAE 0.0149302, a +0.213% change versus persistence (positive means worse). Its mean pooled return RMSE improves by 5.70%, while price MAE changes by +0.312%. Different metrics therefore need separate interpretation. The tolerance rule chooses a practical neural benchmark; it is not a significance test and does not require a neural model to beat persistence. Smooth L1 is not the MAE selection objective: better performance on large return errors can coexist with worse typical absolute or price errors.

Dropout at fixed widths. Adding dropout 0.1 to 64 → 32 changes mean date-MAE by -0.309% relative to the no-dropout model. BatchNorm at fixed widths. Adding BatchNorm to the dropout model changes mean date-MAE by +0.024%. Positive changes mean higher error. Paired seed differences preserve the individual comparisons; three seeds are insufficient for a broad claim about superiority.

Depth and width. Shallow, compact, wide, deep, and residual results test useful practical structures, but capacity and optimization change together. Despite similar parameter counts (1,089 versus 1,057), the shallow model's mean date-MAE changes by +1.392% relative to compact. The four-hidden-layer model changes date-MAE by +0.030% relative to the tapered wide model (13,569 versus 12,417 parameters). The residual model changes it by +0.183% relative to wide, using 17,985 parameters. These are measured common-recipe comparisons, not claims about all possible widths or tuning budgets. A network's additional operations are justified only if the observed accuracy benefit merits the cost in the next table.

Deep has the lowest neural mean price MAE and price RMSE in this study. Its price RMSE changes by -0.0615% versus persistence, while its date-MAE and price MAE remain worse. This small secondary-metric gain does not change the predeclared date-MAE selection.

Low option prices can turn a small absolute price change into a large log return. The selected model's seed-42 return RMSE changes by -8.02% versus persistence for prices at or below one, and -0.10% above one. Comparing both subsets helps determine whether pooled return RMSE is dominated by cheap options. Low-price rows are 2.29% of validation observations but contribute 63.60% of persistence's squared return error and 99.25% of the selected seed-42 model's squared-error reduction. The overall RMSE gain is therefore concentrated in cheap options. The zero-volume validation subset contains only 2 observations and cannot support a meaningful volume-regime conclusion. 7. Other deep learning techniques and the next experiment

The following techniques were researched but not trained in this study. Their performance and timing on Nifty remain unmeasured.

Technique Expected value to test Compute and inference implications
LSTM Learn temporal order that six-bar summaries discard; no guaranteed accuracy gain Requires causal per-bar sequence inputs; recurrent time steps introduce sequential work
Causal TCN Learn short local patterns and a chosen receptive field using temporal convolutions Requires sequence inputs; convolutions parallelize positions, with cost set by channels, kernels, and depth
FT-Transformer Learn interactions among the existing tabular features through feature tokens Can use the current features; embeddings and attention add work, with quadratic attention interactions in token count

These proposals follow Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997), Bai et al. (2018), and Gorishniy et al. (2021). The original Transformer paper explains attention's token-count cost and parallelism. With short windows or few feature tokens, measured overhead and hidden width also matter. Published rankings on other datasets do not predict the ranking on these option returns.

For the next check-in, retain persistence and the selected compact neural benchmark, improve the causal sequence representation before adding an LSTM/TCN, and use validation-only date/regime checks. A later final comparison should freeze choices first and evaluate the reserved test partition once. No claim of profitable trading follows from the current error metrics.

9. Short check-in answer

Based on last week's exploration, we used 15 cleaned features to predict next-minute Nifty option log returns. We compared eight MLP architectures—including shallow, deeper, dropout, BatchNorm, and residual networks—across three seeds, with persistence and ridge as reference baselines. The wide MLP achieved the lowest neural validation date-MAE (0.0149090), while our size-versus-accuracy rule selected the compact MLP (1,057 parameters; approximately 49 µs warm CPU inference per example). The best MLP's mean date-MAE was 0.07% higher than persistence. We will retain this small neural benchmark and investigate sequence models next; these are validation findings, with final test evaluation still reserved.
